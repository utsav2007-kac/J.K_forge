Tooling Ownership and Tooling Care Are Two Different Questions

When a customer pays for and owns the forging dies used to produce their components — a common arrangement, particularly for high-volume or long-running programs where the customer wants tooling ownership rights, control over tooling relocation, or simply wants the tooling capitalized on their own books rather than the supplier's — ownership answers the question of who holds title to the tooling, but it doesn't answer the equally important operational question of who's responsible for keeping that tooling in good working condition day to day. Left informal, this gap tends to produce exactly the outcome both sides want to avoid: tooling wear goes untracked until a die starts producing marginal or nonconforming parts, refurbishment gets scheduled reactively after a problem rather than proactively before one, and the customer, as the tooling's legal owner, has limited real visibility into the actual condition and remaining service life of an asset they paid for but don't handle day to day. A structured COT management program closes this gap by formalizing what happens to customer-owned tooling between production runs — documented storage conditions protecting the tooling from corrosion or damage when not in active use, a preventive maintenance schedule rather than a purely reactive one, die life and cycle count tracking giving the customer actual visibility into remaining tooling life, and a clear, mutually understood process for when refurbishment or replacement becomes necessary — turning tooling care from an informal assumption into a documented program both sides can rely on.

Customer-Owned Tooling Program Elements

Structured Tooling Storage

Customer-owned dies and tooling stored under controlled conditions protecting against corrosion, physical damage, and misidentification when not in active production use, with tooling tracked by identification number against the customer's own asset records.

Preventive Maintenance Scheduling

Tooling maintenance scheduled proactively based on cycle count and condition rather than performed only reactively once a die begins producing marginal or out-of-tolerance parts.

Die Life and Cycle Count Tracking

Production cycle counts logged against each tooling set, giving the customer, as tooling owner, actual documented visibility into cumulative usage and estimated remaining service life rather than an informal estimate.

Refurbishment and Replacement Scheduling

A defined process and communicated trigger point for scheduling die refurbishment or replacement before wear reaches a level that compromises part quality, discussed with the customer ahead of the need becoming urgent.

Program Structure and Customer Communication

Tooling Condition Reporting

Periodic reporting to the customer on tooling condition, cumulative cycle count, and any maintenance or refurbishment activity performed, keeping the tooling owner informed of their asset's status without requiring them to inspect it directly.

Tooling Relocation and Handover Support

Documented tooling condition and history supporting a clean handover if the customer elects to relocate tooling to another supplier, consistent with the customer's ownership rights over tooling they've paid for.

Agreed Maintenance and Refurbishment Responsibility

Clear upfront agreement on which maintenance and refurbishment costs are covered under ongoing production pricing versus billed separately, avoiding ambiguity when a refurbishment need arises.

Tooling Identification and Asset Reconciliation

Tooling identified and tracked in a way that supports periodic reconciliation against the customer's own fixed asset records, useful for customers who capitalize tooling on their own books.

Tooling Ownership and Tooling Care Are Two Different Questions

Customer-owned tooling arrangements are common in forging supply relationships for good reason — a customer who pays for and owns the dies producing their components gains ownership rights, control over tooling relocation, and often a capitalization benefit on their own books that a supplier-owned tooling arrangement doesn't offer. But ownership of a physical asset and day-to-day responsibility for that asset's condition are genuinely separate questions, and when a supply relationship leaves the second question informal — assumed rather than documented — the gap tends to surface at the worst possible time: as a die nearing the end of its practical service life without either party having tracked that fact, producing marginal or nonconforming parts before the wear condition becomes obvious enough to prompt action.

A structured customer-owned tooling management program exists specifically to close this gap, converting tooling care from an informal assumption embedded in the supply relationship into a documented, proactive program with clear responsibilities on both sides. This starts with basic asset stewardship — tooling stored under controlled conditions that protect against corrosion and physical damage between production runs, and identified and tracked in a way that supports reconciliation against the customer's own fixed asset records — but the more operationally significant element is shifting maintenance from reactive to preventive: scheduling upkeep based on tracked cycle count and periodic condition assessment, rather than waiting for a die to begin producing parts outside tolerance before addressing wear that had, in reality, been accumulating gradually and predictably all along.

The tracking component of a COT program is what gives the customer, as the actual tooling owner, genuine visibility into an asset they don't handle directly day to day: cumulative cycle counts logged against each tooling set translate into an estimated remaining service life that can be reported on a regular basis, turning refurbishment or replacement planning into a scheduled, budgeted activity discussed ahead of need rather than an urgent, unplanned disruption to a production program once a die's condition degrades to the point of affecting part quality. This proactive visibility is, in practice, the core value a formal tooling management program delivers beyond simple physical custody of the tooling itself.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is customer-owned tooling (COT)?

Customer-owned tooling refers to forging dies and related tooling that the customer pays for and legally owns, but which is held and used at the supplier's facility for ongoing production of that customer's components — a common arrangement for high-volume or long-running programs where the customer wants ownership rights or wants the tooling capitalized on their own books.

Why does customer-owned tooling need a formal management program?

Because ownership and day-to-day care are separate questions. Without a structured program, tooling wear can go untracked until a die starts producing marginal parts, and the customer — despite legally owning the tooling — has limited real visibility into its actual condition. A formal program gives the customer documented insight into storage condition, maintenance history, and remaining service life.

Who pays for tooling maintenance and refurbishment under a COT program?

This is agreed upfront as part of setting up the program — some maintenance may be included in ongoing production pricing, while more significant refurbishment or die replacement is typically billed separately. We establish this clearly at program setup specifically to avoid ambiguity when a refurbishment need eventually arises.

Can we relocate our tooling to another supplier if needed?

Yes — as the tooling owner, you retain that right. A documented tooling condition and maintenance history under our COT program supports a clean, well-informed handover if you elect to relocate tooling, since the receiving party has an accurate record of the tooling's actual condition rather than an unknown.

How do you track remaining die life?

Production cycle counts are logged against each tooling set over time, and combined with periodic condition assessment, this gives an estimated remaining service life that's reported to the customer rather than left as an informal, undocumented estimate — supporting proactive refurbishment planning instead of reactive response to a failing die.
